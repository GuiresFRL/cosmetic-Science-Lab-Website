Panthenol (provitamin B5): what the evidence shows for skin barrier

Panthenol is a gentle humectant with decades of use in barrier and recovery care. CSL formulation scientists look at what recent studies show, where the evidence is thin, and how to use it well.

By CSL Formulation team · Evidence checked by CSL scientists

Ingredient guideEvidence: Moderate

At a glance

What it is
The alcohol form of pantothenic acid (vitamin B5). Dexpanthenol (D-panthenol) is the active form; DL-panthenol is the mixed form
Main benefits
Better-looking hydration, a stronger-feeling skin barrier, less visible redness after procedures, softer, easier-to-comb hair
Levels tested in trials
0.5% to 5% in skin studies, most often 2.5% to 5%; level not disclosed in recent mask studies
Time to results
Hydration within days; barrier and redness changes over 1 to 2 weeks after procedures
Watch for
Most recent studies test whole products, not panthenol alone; rare allergic contact reactions; medicine status of some 5% ointments

01What is it?

Panthenol is a clear, water-soluble provitamin of vitamin B5. Skin converts it to pantothenic acid, a building block of coenzyme A. It has been used in skin and hair care for more than 70 years and is one of the most widely used soothing and conditioning ingredients.

02What it does

Panthenol attracts and holds water in the outer skin layer and helps the barrier lipids stay mobile. Human biopsy work shows it changes the activity of genes involved in skin repair. On skin this shows as better hydration, lower water loss and calmer-looking skin; on hair it acts as a humectant and antistatic conditioner.

03The evidence

Strength of human evidenceModerate: consistent but mostly small, older controlled trials, and recent studies test multi-ingredient products

CSL verdict

Panthenol is a safe, well-tolerated humectant with a long record of improving skin hydration and helping the barrier recover, but most trials are small, older and often industry-funded, and recent studies test complete products. Claims such as “helps support the skin barrier”, “soothes the look of redness” and “improves hydration” are reasonable when supported by testing of the finished product.

04How CSL formulation scientists use it

  • ☐Most skin studies use 2.5% to 5%. CSL formulation scientists typically use 1% to 5% in leave-on skin care and lower levels for hair care, where it acts as a humectant and antistatic conditioner.
  • ☐Panthenol is water-soluble, works in clear gels, serums, creams and masks, and is compatible with most actives. It is best kept in a mild pH range (roughly 4 to 7) and added below high-heat stages to avoid breakdown.
  • ☐Dexpanthenol (D-panthenol) is the biologically active form; DL-panthenol contains only half as much of it. Claims should reflect the form used.
  • ☐It pairs well with ceramides, glycerin, niacinamide and centella in barrier and post-procedure products. Allergic contact reactions are rare but reported, so tolerance testing on sensitive skin is still advised.

05Regulatory and claims

The US Cosmetic Ingredient Review panel (2022) concluded panthenol and related ingredients are safe as used in cosmetics, with reported uses up to about 5% in body, hand and hair products and 2.5% in baby lotions and creams. No specific EU restriction is listed in the Cosmetics Europe ingredient database, which names its functions as antistatic, hair conditioning and skin conditioning; CSL regulatory specialists confirm the status in each target market. Some 5% dexpanthenol ointments for wounds and nappy rash are sold as medicines, so claims must stay cosmetic: “soothes and protects the look of dry, irritated skin”, not “heals wounds” or “treats nappy rash”.

06Questions brands ask

Is panthenol the same as vitamin B5?

Nearly. Panthenol is the provitamin form; skin converts it into pantothenic acid (vitamin B5). It is more stable and penetrates skin better than the vitamin itself.

What level of panthenol works?

Studies show benefits from 0.5%, with 2.5% to 5% the most tested. Around 5% tends to give the strongest results in skin trials.

Is panthenol suitable for sensitive and baby skin?

Generally yes. It has a long record of good tolerance in babies and adults, including in nappy-area products. Rare allergic reactions have been reported, so finished products should still be tolerance-tested.
