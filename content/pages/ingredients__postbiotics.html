Postbiotic lysates: what the evidence shows for calmer, stronger skin

Postbiotics are inactivated bacteria or their by-products. Some have placebo-controlled trials for reactive and dry skin. Here is what they show and how CSL formulation scientists use them.

By CSL Formulation team · Evidence checked by CSL scientists

Ingredient guideEvidence: Moderate

At a glance

What it is
Non-living bacterial material: lysates of Vitreoscilla filiformis, Lactobacillus and related strains, or their metabolites
Main benefits
Calmer-looking, less reactive skin; better barrier and hydration readings; fewer visible blemishes in some trials
Levels tested in trials
2% to 30% of the supplier ingredient; 5% Vitreoscilla filiformis lysate; 2% Bacillus coagulans metabolite; 3% Lactobacillus lysate blend
Time to results
3 to 10 weeks in controlled studies
Watch for
Results are strain- and supplier-specific; most trials are small and industry-funded; one 2026 trial found no benefit

01What is it?

Postbiotics are bacteria that have been inactivated, broken open (lysed) or filtered to keep their by-products. They contain no live microbes, which makes them easier to formulate and preserve than probiotics. Each postbiotic comes from a specific strain, so results from one cannot be applied to another.

02What it does

Bacterial fragments and metabolites seem to talk to the skin's immune and barrier systems, calming reactivity and supporting the barrier. In trials this showed as less itching and redness, lower water loss, and in some acne studies fewer spots. Effects are usually moderate and appear within a few weeks.

03The evidence

Strength of human evidenceModerate: a few placebo-controlled trials with positive results, but small, short, mostly industry-funded and not always repeated

CSL verdict

Some postbiotics, especially Vitreoscilla filiformis lysate, have placebo-controlled evidence for calmer, better-protected skin, but the evidence is strain-specific, small and mostly industry-funded, and a 2026 trial found no benefit. Suitable claims are modest, such as “helps reduce the look of redness” or “supports the skin barrier”, tied to the exact ingredient and level tested.

04How CSL formulation scientists use it

  • ☐Use the supplier's tested strain at the level used in its trials, commonly 2% to 5% of the supplied ingredient. A different strain needs its own evidence.
  • ☐Postbiotics contain no live bacteria, so they fit standard preserved emulsions and serums. Check pH range, heat limits and colour or odour drift in stability testing.
  • ☐They pair well with barrier ingredients such as ceramides, glycerin and niacinamide in products for reactive, dry or blemish-prone skin.
  • ☐Ask suppliers for identity, strain name, inactivation method and microbiological specifications, plus any human data on the ingredient.

05Regulatory and claims

Bacterial lysates are used as cosmetic ingredients under INCI names such as Vitreoscilla ferment, Lactobacillus ferment lysate and Bifida ferment lysate. CSL regulatory specialists confirm the status in each target market before a formula is finalised. Avoid calling a product “probiotic” when it contains no live microbes, and keep claims cosmetic: “calms the look of sensitive skin”, not “treats eczema”.

06Questions brands ask

Is a postbiotic the same as a probiotic?

No. A probiotic contains live bacteria. A postbiotic contains inactivated bacteria or their by-products, which is why it can go into normal preserved products.

Do all bacterial lysates work the same way?

No. Effects depend on the strain and how it was made. Vitreoscilla filiformis lysate has the longest record, but a 2026 trial of a Lactobacillus lactis lysate found no benefit in atopic skin.

Can postbiotics replace acne actives?

Not on current evidence. Reviews suggest they may help alongside proven actives, but trials are small and short.
