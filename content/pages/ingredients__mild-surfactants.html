Mild surfactants: what the evidence shows for gentler cleansing

Amino-acid surfactants, glucosides, betaines and biosurfactants are sold as gentler than SLS. Here is what human studies show, where the evidence is thin, and how CSL formulation scientists build mild cleansers.

By CSL Formulation team · Evidence checked by CSL scientists

Ingredient guideEvidence: Moderate

At a glance

What it is
Cleansing agents chosen for low irritation: amino-acid types (cocoyl glutamate, cocoyl glycinate, lauroyl sarcosinate), alkyl glucosides, amphoteric betaines and glycolipid biosurfactants, compared with SLS and SLES
Main benefits
Effective cleansing with less dryness, tightness and barrier disruption than harsh anionic systems
Levels tested in trials
Tested as finished cleansers rather than single ingredients; individual surfactant levels are rarely reported
Time to results
Mildness shows within days of repeated washing; skin comfort in dry or atopic skin assessed over 4 weeks
Watch for
Contact allergy to alkyl glucosides and to cocamidopropyl betaine impurities; 'mild' depends on the whole formula, not one ingredient

01What is it?

Surfactants are the cleaning ingredients in body washes, shampoos and facial cleansers. Sodium lauryl sulphate (SLS) is a strong, well-studied skin irritant and is used as the benchmark for harshness. 'Mild surfactants' is a loose group of alternatives: amino-acid surfactants, sugar-based alkyl glucosides, amphoteric betaines and newer fermentation-derived biosurfactants such as sophorolipids. Most are used in blends, often with a smaller amount of a stronger anionic surfactant.

02What it does

Harsh anionic surfactants bind to and swell skin proteins and strip barrier lipids, which shows as dryness, redness and raised water loss after repeated washing. Milder types, and blends with betaines or polymers, form larger, less penetrating micelles and interact less with proteins. In practice this means cleansers that remove dirt and make-up while leaving the skin barrier closer to its unwashed state.

03The evidence

Strength of human evidenceModerate: one good randomised trial of an SLS-free wash and several uncontrolled or industry studies; few head-to-head trials of single surfactants

CSL verdict

There is fair evidence that SLS-free cleansers built on amino-acid, glucoside and betaine blends are well tolerated, even on newborn and eczema-prone skin. Head-to-head human trials of single mild surfactants against SLS are scarce, and many studies are uncontrolled or industry-run. Suitable claims are 'gentle cleansing', 'sulphate-free' and 'helps maintain the skin's natural moisture barrier', backed by the finished product's own tests.

04How CSL formulation scientists use it

  • ☐Mildness belongs to the whole system: CSL formulation scientists blend a primary amino-acid or glucoside surfactant with a betaine, and may add polymers that enlarge micelles and reduce barrier damage (Walters et al. 2012).
  • ☐Glutamates and glycinates are pH-sensitive and often need a pH near neutral to stay clear and foam well; glucosides are high-pH raw materials that must be adjusted, and they thicken poorly, so rheology modifiers are planned early.
  • ☐Each 'mild' claim is checked on the finished product with repeat-wash or patch testing, because a mild surfactant at the wrong level or in the wrong blend can still be drying.
  • ☐For sensitive-skin or 'hypoallergenic' ranges, CSL formulation scientists weigh the known contact allergy to alkyl glucosides and to cocamidopropyl betaine impurities, and request low-impurity betaine grades from suppliers.

05Regulatory and claims

The common mild surfactants are not restricted by concentration in the main markets as far as CSL could confirm; CSL regulatory specialists confirm the status of each raw material in every target market. Cocamidopropyl betaine was the American Contact Dermatitis Society Allergen of the Year in 2004 and alkyl glucosides in 2017 (not 2023, which was lanolin). 'Sulphate-free' and 'gentle' claims must be truthful and supported by product testing, and should stay cosmetic: 'gentle on sensitive skin', not 'treats eczema'.

06Questions brands ask

Is sulphate-free always milder?

Not automatically. SLS is a known irritant, but mildness depends on the full blend, its level and pH. A well-built SLES and betaine blend can be milder than a poorly built sulphate-free one.

Are glucosides safe for sensitive skin?

They are gentle as cleansers, but decyl and lauryl glucoside cause contact allergy in a small share of people patch tested (around 1–2% in some clinic series), which is why they were named Allergen of the Year in 2017.

Is there human evidence for biosurfactants like sophorolipids?

Not yet in cleansing trials. Lab skin-model studies look favourable compared with SLES, but claims should wait for human testing of the finished product.
