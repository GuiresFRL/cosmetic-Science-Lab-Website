Salicylic acid: what the evidence shows for blemishes, pores and scalp

Salicylic acid is the classic oil-soluble exfoliant for blemish-prone skin and flaky scalps. CSL formulation scientists review what recent trials show and where the evidence is thinner than its reputation.

By CSL Formulation team · Evidence checked by CSL scientists

Ingredient guideEvidence: Moderate

At a glance

What it is
A beta hydroxy acid (BHA) that dissolves in oil, so it can work inside oily pores
Main benefits
Fewer visible blemishes and blackheads, clearer-looking pores, less visible flaking on the scalp
Levels tested in trials
2% leave-on gel; 30% in-clinic peels; shampoos in combination with other anti-dandruff actives
Time to results
2 to 12 weeks in the studies reviewed
Watch for
Dryness and stinging, pH-dependent activity, age and product-type limits in some markets

01What is it?

Salicylic acid is a beta hydroxy acid. Unlike glycolic or lactic acid, it dissolves in oil, so it can reach the sebum-filled openings of pores. It is one of the most widely used ingredients in products for blemish-prone skin and in anti-dandruff shampoos.

02What it does

Salicylic acid loosens the bonds between dead skin cells, helping them shed rather than clog pores. It also has a mild calming effect. On the face this shows as fewer visible blackheads and spots; on the scalp it helps lift visible flakes so other anti-dandruff actives can work.

03The evidence

Strength of human evidenceModerate: one large randomised trial and supporting studies, but few recent vehicle-controlled trials and many combination products

CSL verdict

Salicylic acid is a sensible, well-tolerated choice for blemish-prone skin, and a 2% gel performed about as well as a retinoid gel in a large trial. But recent vehicle-controlled data at cosmetic leave-on levels are scarce, and much of the evidence comes from peels or combination products. Suitable claims are “helps unclog pores” and “reduces the look of blemishes”.

04How CSL formulation scientists use it

  • ☐Salicylic acid has a pKa of about 3, so the share of free, active acid depends on pH. CSL formulation scientists set pH to balance exfoliating activity against tolerability, and test both.
  • ☐It is poorly soluble in water, so formulas need a suitable solvent system (for example glycols or alcohol) or a carrier technology; check for crystallisation in stability testing at low temperatures.
  • ☐Leave-on and rinse-off products behave differently: shampoos and cleansers have short contact times, so claims should be based on tests of the finished format.
  • ☐Pair with soothing and barrier-supporting ingredients, and take care when layering with retinoids or other acids. Salicylates are not suitable for people with a known salicylate allergy.

05Regulatory and claims

Salicylic acid is a regulated ingredient. In the EU it appears in Annex III (restricted substances) of the Cosmetics Regulation with product-type limits and conditions of use, and in the US it is an over-the-counter acne drug active when acne claims are made. CSL regulatory specialists confirm the status in each target market, including the permitted level for the product type and any age restrictions, before a formula is finalised. Claims stay cosmetic: “helps clear the look of blemishes”, not “treats acne”.

06Questions brands ask

Is 2% salicylic acid better than 0.5%?

Most clinical data use about 2% in leave-on products. Lower levels may still help, but there is little direct trial evidence comparing strengths in cosmetic formats. The permitted maximum also varies by market and product type.

Can salicylic acid be used on sensitive skin?

Often, yes. In the large 2024 trial side effects were rare. It can still cause dryness or stinging, so CSL formulation scientists balance pH and add soothing ingredients.

Does salicylic acid work in shampoo?

It helps loosen visible flakes, and it is common in anti-dandruff shampoos. Recent studies test it with other actives such as selenium sulfide, so its own contribution is hard to measure.
