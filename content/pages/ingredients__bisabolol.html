Bisabolol in skin care: what the evidence shows for soothing skin

Bisabolol is a popular soothing ingredient from chamomile and candeia, but human trials of it on its own are few. CSL formulation scientists explain what the evidence does and does not support.

By CSL Formulation team · Evidence checked by CSL scientists

Ingredient guideEvidence: Limited

At a glance

What it is
Alpha-bisabolol (levomenol), a sesquiterpene alcohol from German chamomile and the candeia tree, also made synthetically
Main benefits
Calmer, more comfortable-looking skin; a small brightening effect in one controlled trial; helps other ingredients enter the skin
Levels tested in trials
0.5% alone in the only vehicle-controlled trial; unstated levels in combination products; typical use 0.1% to 1%
Time to results
2 to 8 weeks in the human studies found
Watch for
Rare allergy, mainly in people allergic to the daisy (Compositae) family; it can raise penetration of other ingredients

01What is it?

Bisabolol is an oily, almost colourless liquid found in chamomile essential oil and in candeia wood oil. Most cosmetic grades are the (−)-alpha form, either natural or nature-identical synthetic. It is used at low levels, usually well under 1%, as a soothing and comfort ingredient in creams, lip care and baby products.

02What it does

Laboratory and animal studies show that bisabolol damps down inflammatory signalling, which is the basis for its soothing reputation. It also loosens the lipids between skin cells, so it helps other ingredients pass into the skin. In people, the clearest result is a small reduction in measured skin colour over 8 weeks; the soothing benefit has been seen only in products that also contain other calming ingredients.

03The evidence

Strength of human evidenceLimited: one small vehicle-controlled trial; most human data come from uncontrolled studies of multi-ingredient products

CSL verdict

Bisabolol is a well-tolerated comfort ingredient with a long safety record, but the human evidence for it working on its own is thin: one small trial for brightening and none that isolate its soothing effect. Claims should stay modest and product-based, such as "helps soothe the look of dry, uncomfortable skin" or "formulated with bisabolol", supported by testing of the finished product.

04How CSL formulation scientists use it

  • ☐Typical use is 0.1% to 1%; the CIR assessment reported leave-on use up to 1%, and 0.5% is the level tested in the only vehicle-controlled trial.
  • ☐It is oil-soluble and fairly stable, so it is added to the oil phase or at the end of emulsions, and works well in lip care, baby care and after-sun products.
  • ☐Because it increases skin penetration, CSL formulation scientists review the whole formula, especially retinoids, acids, fragrance and preservatives, and confirm tolerance with finished-product testing.
  • ☐Source matters: confirm the grade (natural chamomile or candeia, or synthetic), its (−)-alpha-bisabolol content and its sustainability claims with the supplier.

05Regulatory and claims

The Cosmetic Ingredient Review Expert Panel concluded in 1999 that bisabolol is safe as used in cosmetics, and the conclusion was re-reviewed in 2015. CSL regulatory specialists confirm the status in each target market. Claims are kept cosmetic, for example "calms the look of redness" or "comforts dry lips", never "treats eczema" or "cures dermatitis".

06Questions brands ask

Is bisabolol proven to calm sensitive skin?

Not on its own. It calms inflammation in laboratory studies, and products containing it have helped dry lips and childhood eczema, but those products also contained other soothing ingredients and had no comparison group.

Does bisabolol brighten skin?

There is a small signal. One 8-week trial in 28 women found 0.5% bisabolol lightened measured skin colour more than the base cream, but the dermatologist's assessment did not differ significantly. It is best treated as a supporting ingredient, not a main brightener.

Can people with plant allergies use it?

Most can. Allergy is rare, but it has been reported mainly in people already allergic to the daisy family, which includes chamomile. Brands targeting very sensitive or atopic skin should include it in finished-product safety testing.
