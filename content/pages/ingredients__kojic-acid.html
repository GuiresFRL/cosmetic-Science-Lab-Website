Kojic acid: what the evidence shows for dark spots and uneven tone

Kojic acid is a long-used brightening active with small trials and a new EU limit of 1%. CSL formulation scientists explain what the evidence supports and how to keep it stable.

By CSL Formulation team · Evidence checked by CSL scientists

Ingredient guideEvidence: Moderate

At a glance

What it is
A natural tyrosinase inhibitor made by fungi during fermentation (for example in rice for sake); also used as the oil-soluble ester kojic dipalmitate
Main benefits
Lighter-looking dark spots and a more even-looking tone, usually as part of a combination
Levels tested in trials
0.75% to 4% in clinical studies, mostly 2%; often combined with arbutin, niacinamide, vitamin C or glycolic acid
Time to results
8 to 16 weeks in controlled studies
Watch for
EU maximum of 1% in face and hand products; contact allergy; browning and colour change in the pack

01What is it?

Kojic acid is a small molecule produced by fungi such as Aspergillus during fermentation. It has been used in skin-lightening cosmetics for decades, especially in Asia. Kojic dipalmitate is an ester that is more stable and oil-soluble, but it has very little published clinical data of its own.

02What it does

Kojic acid binds copper in tyrosinase, the enzyme that starts melanin production, so less pigment is made. Over two to four months this can show as lighter-looking dark spots and a more even-looking tone. In most trials it is combined with other brightening actives, so its own share of the effect is hard to separate.

03The evidence

Strength of human evidenceModerate: several small comparative trials, mostly with mixed formulas and no vehicle control; generally less effective than hydroquinone

CSL verdict

Kojic acid gives a real but modest brightening effect, and in recent small trials combination formulas with it performed close to stronger comparators with fewer side effects. It is less effective than hydroquinone on its own, and most data come from mixed formulas, so claims should stay modest, such as “reduces the look of dark spots” and “evens the look of skin tone”, supported by testing of the finished product at the level used.

04How CSL formulation scientists use it

  • ☐For the EU, CSL formulation scientists design face and hand products at or below 1% kojic acid; many trials used 2–4%, so efficacy at 1% should be shown on the finished product rather than borrowed from those studies.
  • ☐Kojic acid tends to discolour (yellow to brown) with light, heat and trace metals such as iron. Chelating agents, antioxidants, opaque or airless packs and real-time stability testing help keep colour and activity.
  • ☐It works best alongside actives that act at other steps of pigmentation, such as niacinamide, arbutin, tranexamic acid or vitamin C, together with daily sun protection.
  • ☐Kojic acid can cause contact allergy. CSL formulation scientists include sensitisation testing (for example, an HRIPT) and avoid positioning it for use straight after peels or on damaged skin, where the SCCS did not consider it safe. Kojic dipalmitate is more stable but has little clinical data and no SCCS safety conclusion.

05Regulatory and claims

In the EU, Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/996 added kojic acid to Annex III of the Cosmetics Regulation (entry 375): maximum 1% in face and hand products. Non-compliant products could not be placed on the EU market from 1 February 2025 or made available from 1 November 2025. This follows SCCS opinions (SCCS/1481/12 and SCCS/1637/21) finding kojic acid safe up to 1% as a skin-lightening agent in leave-on face and hand products; the SCCS drew no conclusion on derivatives such as kojic dipalmitate. For other markets, including ASEAN, CSL regulatory specialists confirm the status in each target market, and claims stay cosmetic: “brighter, more even-looking skin”, not “treats melasma”.

06Questions brands ask

Is kojic acid as effective as hydroquinone?

Not on its own. A 12-week comparison found 4% hydroquinone worked faster and better than a 0.75% kojic acid cream. Recent small trials of kojic acid combined with arbutin or niacinamide came close, with fewer side effects.

Can a product sold in the EU contain more than 1% kojic acid?

No, not in face and hand products. Since 2025 EU law caps kojic acid at 1% in these products, and non-compliant stock had to leave the market by 1 November 2025.

Is kojic dipalmitate a good substitute?

It is easier to keep stable and suits oil-based formats, but there are very few published human trials of it, and the SCCS reached no safety conclusion on it. Any claims need testing of the finished product.
