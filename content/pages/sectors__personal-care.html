The science

The science of everyday care.

Personal care splits into three areas with different skin, efficacy and regulatory needs.

Deodorant & antiperspirant

Fresh for longer, kind to underarms.

Antiperspirants reduce sweat with aluminium salts; deodorants control odour by targeting the bacteria that break sweat down. Both change the underarm microbiome, so we design for efficacy and comfort on freshly shaved or sensitive skin.

Ingredients CSL scientists work with

Aluminium salts (antiperspirant)Zinc ricinoleateMagnesium hydroxidePrebioticsEncapsulated fragranceSoothing agents

How CSL proves it works

  • Gravimetric sweat testSweat weight reduction for antiperspirant claims
  • Odour panelsTrained sniffers at 24, 48 and 72 hours
  • Underarm toleranceIn-use on shaved and sensitive skin
  • StainingMarks on fabric
Published evidenceSwary 2015 · sweat reductionUrban 2016 · underarm microbiome
Market note

Antiperspirants are OTC drugs in the US with fixed actives and labelling; deodorants making odour claims only are cosmetics. In the EU both are cosmetics.

Hand care & hygiene

Clean hands, healthy skin.

Hand hygiene reduces illness in randomised trials, but frequent washing damages skin. Alcohol rubs with emollients are gentler than repeated soap and water, and barrier and care creams help prevent hand dermatitis when used consistently.

Ingredients CSL scientists work with

Mild surfactantsGlycerinEmollients & lipidsAllantoinPanthenolEthanol or isopropanol (sanitisers)

How CSL proves it works

  • Repeat-wash toleranceTEWL and dryness after repeated use
  • HydrationCorneometer on hands
  • EfficacyAntimicrobial tests for sanitiser claims, where regulated
Published evidenceHübner 2010 · office workersAzor-Martinez 2018 · childcareBoyce 2000 · skin toleranceKampf 2005 · emollientsKütting 2010 · barrier creams
Market note

Hand sanitisers are OTC drugs in the US and biocides in the EU, not cosmetics. We develop the formula and prepare the right route, or keep the product cosmetic with cleansing-only claims.

Intimate, foot & lip care

Care for skin that needs special attention.

Intimate skin has a lower pH and its own microbiome, so washes must be mild and pH-balanced. Cracked heels respond to urea in placebo-controlled trials, and lip products with barrier lipids reduce roughness.

Ingredients CSL scientists work with

Lactic acid (pH 3.5–5)Mild amphoteric surfactantsUreaGlycerinPetrolatumCeramides & pseudo-ceramides

  • Gynaecologist-supervised in-useTolerance for intimate washes

Claims note

“Treats thrush”, “prevents infection” or “for diabetic foot ulcers” are medical claims. We keep claims to cleansing, comfort, freshness and dry-skin care.

Clinical evidence

What the published trials show.

Peer-reviewed human studies on deodorants, hand hygiene, hand care, intimate care, foot care and lip care, including one with a negative result. Each links to its record.

Randomised controlled trialAntiperspirant · sweat reduction

20% aluminium chloride hexahydrate vs 1% aluminium acetate, one per underarm

Randomised split-axilla · 19 adults · gravimetric sweat test

The aluminium chloride side sweated significantly less than the comparison side (p=0.0002), showing how gravimetric testing separates actives.

Small study; 20% aluminium chloride is prescription strength in some markets.

Swary JH et al. J Cosmet Dermatol 2015;14(4) ↗

Published studyDeodorant · underarm microbiome

Stopping and restarting deodorant or antiperspirant vs non-users

Experimental study · 17 adults · 8 days · culture and 16S sequencing

Antiperspirant sharply reduced underarm bacterial density, and product users had a different bacterial community from non-users.

Not randomised; no odour endpoint.

Urban J et al. PeerJ 2016;4:e1605 ↗

Randomised controlled trialHand hygiene · office workers

Alcohol hand disinfectant at desks vs usual practice

Randomised controlled · 129 office workers · 12 months

Colds (OR 0.35), fever (OR 0.38) and cough (OR 0.45) were all less frequent, with fewer days of illness.

Hübner NO et al. BMC Infect Dis 2010;10:250 · PMID 20735818 ↗

Randomised controlled trialHand hygiene · childcare

Hand-sanitiser programme vs soap-and-water vs control

Cluster-randomised · 24 day-care centres, 911 children · 8 months

The sanitiser group had 23% fewer respiratory infections and 31% fewer antibiotic prescriptions than control.

Azor-Martinez E et al. Pediatrics 2018;142(5):e20181245 · PMID 30297500 ↗

Systematic reviewHand hygiene · community

Community hand-hygiene interventions

Systematic review and meta-analysis · 30 studies

Better hand hygiene cut gastrointestinal illness by 31% and respiratory illness by 21%; education with plain soap gave the largest benefit.

Aiello AE et al. Am J Public Health 2008;98(8):1372–1381 ↗

Controlled trialHand hygiene · skin tolerance

Alcohol hand gel vs soap-and-water handwashing

Prospective comparison · 32 nurses · 6 weeks

Irritation and dryness rose substantially with soap and water but changed little with the alcohol gel, confirmed by skin-water measurements.

Boyce JM et al. Infect Control Hosp Epidemiol 2000;21(7):442–448 · PMID 10926393 ↗

Controlled trialHand sanitiser · emollients

Hand rub with vs without emollients

Double-blind repeated application · 35 adults · 2 weeks

Redness and dryness scores were lower with the emollient-containing rub (P=0.022).

Kampf G et al. Contact Dermatitis 2005;53(6):344–349 · PMID 16364124 ↗

Randomised controlled trialHand care · barrier creams

Skin care, skin protection, both, or no advice

Four-arm randomised · 1,020 metalworkers · 12 months

Skin worsened in controls, while combined skin care and protection creams gave the largest improvement.

Kütting B et al. Br J Dermatol 2010;162(2):362–370 · PMID 19804591 ↗

Randomised controlled trialHand cream · healthcare workers

Ward cream dispensers with feedback vs usual care

Cluster-randomised · 19 wards, 501 workers · 12 months

No significant difference in hand-eczema severity overall, though mild cases improved more, showing that access alone is not enough.

Primary endpoint not significant.

Soltanipoor M et al. Contact Dermatitis 2019;80(6):365–373 · PMID 30652317 ↗

Randomised controlled trialFoot care · cracked heels

Urea, glycerin and petrolatum foot cream vs placebo cream

Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled · 167 people with diabetes · 4 weeks

Deep open heel fissures remained in 6% vs 24% with placebo (P=0.002), and dryness improved more with the test cream.

Gin H et al. Diabet Med 2017;34(9):1309–1317 · PMID 28627029 ↗

Systematic reviewFoot care · urea

Moisturisers for dry feet in diabetes

Systematic review and meta-analysis · 12 RCTs, 603 participants

Urea creams outperformed standard base creams for dryness at 4 weeks, with high-certainty evidence.

Borgström K et al. Front Clin Diabetes Healthc 2026;7:1922860 ↗

Published studyIntimate care · lactic acid wash

External intimate wash with lactic acid, pH 4.2

Open, gynaecologist-supervised · 36 women · 28 days

No increase in dryness, redness, itch or stinging; moisturisation rose and the vulvar microbiome was unchanged.

No control group; manufacturer-affiliated authors.

Brown ADG et al. Antibiotics 2020;9(2):55 · PMID 32024047 ↗

Controlled trialLip care · dry lips

Lip product with 0.5% or 2% pseudo-ceramide vs 0%

Controlled comparison · 31 women · 4 weeks

The 2% formula significantly reduced lip roughness at 2 and 4 weeks and improved barrier function.

Tamura E et al. Int J Cosmet Sci 2021;43(2):158–164 · PMID 33258166 ↗

These are published studies on ingredients, methods and routines, not on products we have made. A finished product needs its own testing before a claim goes on pack, and we design that study with you. See the full evidence library

Claims review
Cosmetic, drug or biocide?

Antiperspirants and hand sanitisers are OTC drugs in the US; sanitisers are biocides in the EU. Antibacterial claims on washes and wipes can change the route, and intimate or foot products that claim to treat infection are medicines. We confirm the classification before formulating.

FAQ

Personal care & hygiene questions.

Is an antiperspirant a cosmetic?

In the EU and UK, yes. In the US it is an OTC drug with fixed actives, labelling and sweat-reduction testing. A deodorant with odour claims only is a cosmetic in both.

Can you develop a hand sanitiser?

Yes. We formulate it and prepare the right route: an OTC drug in the US, a biocide in the EU. We can also develop gentle cosmetic hand washes.

How do you prove a 48-hour deodorant claim?

With trained odour panels at set time points after application, and gravimetric sweat testing for antiperspirant claims.

Can intimate washes claim to prevent infection?

No. Those are medical claims. Cosmetic intimate washes can claim gentle cleansing, freshness and respect for natural pH.

Who owns the formula?

You do. We sign an NDA first, and the formula and technical file are handed over at the end.
