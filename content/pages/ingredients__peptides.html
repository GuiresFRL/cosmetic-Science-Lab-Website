Peptides in skin care: what the evidence shows for fine lines

Peptides are short amino-acid chains sold for smoother, firmer-looking skin. Here is what the trials show, why most are industry-funded, and how CSL formulation scientists use them.

By CSL Formulation team · Evidence checked by CSL scientists

Ingredient guideEvidence: Moderate

At a glance

What it is
Short amino-acid chains designed to mimic skin signals: signal peptides (palmitoyl pentapeptide-4), carrier peptides (copper tripeptide-1) and neurotransmitter-inhibiting peptides (acetyl hexapeptide-8)
Main benefits
Modest improvement in the look of fine lines, expression lines and skin smoothness
Levels tested in trials
3 ppm palmitoyl pentapeptide-4; 2% acetyl hexapeptide-8 within a blend; many trials do not disclose levels
Time to results
4 to 12 weeks in controlled studies
Watch for
Small, mostly sponsor-run studies; blends that make single-peptide effects hard to judge; limited skin penetration

01What is it?

Peptides are short chains of amino acids, the building blocks of proteins. Cosmetic peptides are made to imitate fragments that skin uses as signals. They fall into three main groups: signal peptides that aim to boost collagen, carrier peptides that deliver trace metals such as copper, and neurotransmitter-inhibiting peptides that aim to relax expression lines.

02What it does

In lab studies, signal peptides encourage skin cells to make more collagen, and neurotransmitter-inhibiting peptides interfere with the nerve signal behind muscle contraction. On skin, the proposed result is smoother, firmer-looking skin. Many peptides are too large or water-loving to pass easily through the skin barrier, which is one reason the clinical effect is usually modest.

03The evidence

Strength of human evidenceModerate: a few small placebo-controlled trials and recent reviews, mostly industry-linked, with modest effects; a 2026 meta-analysis found only two topical RCTs

Studies below were checked against the publisher, DOI or journal record on 26 Sep 2026. We list design and size so you can judge the strength of each result.

CSL verdict

Peptides are well tolerated and a few placebo-controlled trials show modest gains in the look of fine lines, but the studies are small, short and mostly run by ingredient or product makers. Recent trials test blends, so the effect of any single peptide is hard to isolate. Claims such as ‘helps improve the look of fine lines’, backed by testing of the finished product, are appropriate; comparisons with injectable treatments are not.

04How CSL formulation scientists use it

  • ☐Use peptides at the supplier’s tested level and ask for the supplier’s own clinical data, noting whether it was controlled and independent.
  • ☐Peptides are generally water-soluble and sensitive to extreme pH and some preservatives. CSL formulation scientists check compatibility and confirm stability of the peptide in the finished product.
  • ☐Copper peptides give a blue tint and can interact with strong acids and chelating agents, so they are kept apart from low-pH acid products in the same formula.
  • ☐Peptides pair well with humectants, niacinamide and gentle retinoids in multi-benefit serums; claims should reflect the tested product, not each ingredient on its own.

05Regulatory and claims

CSL regulatory specialists confirm the status and any limits for each peptide in each target market. Claims stay cosmetic (‘improves the look of fine lines’); wording such as ‘Botox-like’ or ‘stimulates collagen production’ can move a product towards a medical claim and is avoided.

06Questions brands ask

Do peptides work like Botox?

No. Acetyl hexapeptide-8 is designed to interfere with the same nerve signal, but it is far weaker than an injectable treatment and does not penetrate skin well. Any effect on expression lines is small.

Which peptide has the best evidence?

Palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 and acetyl hexapeptide-8 each have at least one placebo-controlled trial, but both rest on a small number of short studies. Recent trials test blends, so no single peptide clearly stands out.

Why is peptide evidence often industry-funded?

Most cosmetic peptides are patented ingredients, and their suppliers or the brands using them run and publish the studies. Independent, vehicle-controlled trials are rare, so claims should be modest.
