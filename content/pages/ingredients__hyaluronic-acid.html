Hyaluronic acid: what the evidence shows for hydration and fine lines

Hyaluronic acid is the best-known hydrating active in skin care. Here is what controlled trials show, why molecular weight matters, and how CSL formulation scientists use it well.

By CSL Formulation team · Evidence checked by CSL scientists

Ingredient guideEvidence: Moderate

At a glance

What it is
A water-binding sugar polymer found naturally in skin, used in cosmetics as hyaluronic acid or sodium hyaluronate in low, medium and high molecular weights
Main benefits
Better skin hydration; smoother-looking skin; softer look of fine lines with lower-weight forms
Levels tested in trials
0.1% at single weights from 50 to 2000 kDa; 0.5% multi-weight blend (50 to 3000 kDa)
Time to results
Hydration within days to weeks; fine-line changes after about 8 weeks
Watch for
Small, often industry-funded trials; claims that ignore molecular weight; tacky feel at higher levels

01What is it?

Hyaluronic acid is a sugar-based molecule that holds large amounts of water. The skin makes its own, and levels fall with age. In cosmetics it comes in different molecular weights. High-weight forms stay on the surface; lower-weight forms reach further into the outer skin layers.

02What it does

On the surface, hyaluronic acid draws in water and forms a light film that reduces the look of dryness. Lower-weight forms appear to reach deeper into the upper skin, and in trials they gave larger hydration gains and a small reduction in wrinkle depth. The visible result is plumper, smoother-looking skin while the product is in use.

03The evidence

Strength of human evidenceModerate: a few controlled trials show hydration gains, but most are small and a 2026 review found moderate to high risk of bias

CSL verdict

Topical hyaluronic acid reliably improves skin hydration, and lower-weight forms appear to do more than high-weight forms. Evidence for fine lines is limited to small trials, so claims should stay modest: “hydrates skin” and “reduces the look of fine lines caused by dryness”, not “fills wrinkles”.

04How CSL formulation scientists use it

  • ☐Use a blend of molecular weights: high weight for surface hydration and feel, low weight for deeper hydration. Match any weight-specific claim to the grade actually used.
  • ☐Trials used 0.1% to 0.5%. Above about 1% most grades turn viscous or tacky, so higher is rarely better.
  • ☐Hyaluronic acid pulls water from its surroundings. Pair it with an emollient or occlusive layer so moisture is held in the skin, especially in dry climates.
  • ☐It is stable across a broad pH range and compatible with most actives. Watch for thickening or loss of viscosity with high electrolyte loads and some preservatives, and check this in stability testing.

05Regulatory and claims

Hyaluronic acid and sodium hyaluronate are widely used cosmetic ingredients. CSL regulatory specialists confirm the status in each target market for the exact grade, including any low-weight or cross-linked form. Claims stay cosmetic: “hydrates” and “improves the look of fine lines”, never “repairs” or “regenerates” skin.

06Questions brands ask

Does molecular weight really matter?

Yes. In controlled trials, lower-weight forms gave larger hydration gains, and only the lowest weights reduced wrinkle depth. High-weight forms mainly hydrate the surface.

Does a cream work like a hyaluronic acid filler?

No. Injected fillers add volume under the skin. A topical product hydrates the outer layers and smooths the look of dryness-related lines.

What percentage should a serum contain?

Trials show benefits at 0.1% to 0.5%. A well-chosen blend at these levels is usually better than a high percentage of a single grade.
