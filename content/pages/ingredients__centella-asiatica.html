Centella asiatica: what the evidence shows for calming and skin repair

Centella asiatica, also called cica, is a popular soothing botanical. Here is what the small human studies show, how strong the evidence is, and how CSL formulation scientists use it in calming products.

By CSL Formulation team · Evidence checked by CSL scientists

Ingredient guideEvidence: Moderate

At a glance

What it is
A plant extract (gotu kola, cica) and its active triterpenes, mainly madecassoside and asiaticoside
Main benefits
Calmer, less red-looking skin; support for the skin barrier and hydration; better-looking healing skin after procedures
Levels tested in trials
0.05% standardised extract gel; 2.5% to 5% extract; 7% extract cream; undisclosed levels in combination creams
Time to results
Days for redness after procedures; 2 to 4 weeks for hydration and barrier; up to 12 weeks for scar appearance
Watch for
Variable extract quality; small, mostly single-centre studies; occasional allergic reactions

01What is it?

Centella asiatica is a creeping herb used for centuries in Asian traditional medicine for wounds. Cosmetic extracts are standardised to its triterpenes: madecassoside, asiaticoside and their acids. Products marketed as "cica" usually contain one or more of these.

02What it does

The triterpenes calm inflammation, support collagen production and help the skin barrier recover. In small studies this shows as less redness after laser treatment, better-looking scars, higher skin hydration and lower water loss.

03The evidence

Strength of human evidenceModerate: a few small randomised trials and reviews that describe the human data as small and varied

CSL verdict

Centella asiatica has plausible biology and small, positive human studies for calming redness and supporting barrier recovery. The evidence is too thin for strong claims. Wording such as "helps soothe the look of redness" and "supports the skin barrier" fits; tested finished-product claims are best.

04How CSL formulation scientists use it

  • ☐Specify the material: a standardised extract or pure madecassoside or asiaticoside gives more consistent results than a generic extract. Trials used 0.05% standardised extract up to 5% to 7% crude extract.
  • ☐Madecassoside is water-soluble and suits serums, gels and light creams; asiatic and madecassic acids are less soluble and need careful solubilising.
  • ☐Pairs well with barrier ingredients such as ceramides and panthenol, which is how most recent calming products use it. Support any claim with testing of the finished product.
  • ☐Check botanical raw material for contaminants and microbial load, and run patch testing: allergic reactions, though uncommon, were reported in trials.

05Regulatory and claims

Centella asiatica extracts and their triterpenes are used widely in cosmetics. No specific EU restriction was confirmed in this review; CSL regulatory specialists confirm the status in each target market. Wound and scar healing are medical claims, so cosmetic wording should stay with appearance and comfort, such as "helps calm the look of redness", never "heals wounds" or "treats scars".

06Questions brands ask

Is madecassoside the same as Centella asiatica?

Madecassoside is one of the main active compounds in the plant. Some products use the purified compound, others a whole extract; results depend on which and how much.

Does Centella repair the skin barrier?

Small studies show higher hydration and lower water loss with Centella creams. The best recent data come from combination products, so its own effect is hard to separate.

Is Centella suitable for sensitive skin?

Generally yes, and it is well tolerated in studies. Allergic reactions are uncommon but have been reported, so finished products should be patch tested.
