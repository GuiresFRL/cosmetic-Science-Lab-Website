The science

The skin science behind a better shower.

A body wash touches the whole body every day, so small differences in mildness and moisture add up. We design around three areas.

Mild cleansing

Clean skin, barrier intact.

Alkaline soaps and harsh surfactants lift skin’s own lipids and raise its pH, which can leave it tight and dry and shift its microbial balance. Synthetic detergents (syndets) and mild surfactant blends at skin-friendly pH clean well with far less disruption.

Formulation note

Mildness and foam pull against each other. We blend primary and co-surfactants and use foam boosters so a sulphate-free wash still lathers the way people expect.

Moisture in the shower

Care that stays after you rinse.

Most of a wash goes down the drain within seconds. Moisturising washes and shower oils are built to leave a thin layer of lipids and humectants on skin, and in-shower lotions apply care onto wet skin so people skip a step.

Formulation note

A “moisturising” wash claim needs a measured benefit after rinsing, compared with a standard cleanser. We set that comparison up before the formula is final.

Dry & sensitive skin

Comfort for skin that needs more.

Dry, rough and sensitive body skin needs care beyond the shower. Body lotions and creams built on humectants, natural moisturising factors and barrier lipids restore comfort and keep water in for longer.

High urea levels work well on very dry skin but can sting. We balance urea with glycerin and soothing agents, and test stinging on sensitive panels.

Clinical evidence

What the published trials show.

Peer-reviewed human studies on cleansing, moisturising and scent. Each links to its PubMed record.

Controlled trialMild cleansing · syndet vs soap

Soap vs synthetic detergent (syndet) washing

Controlled crossover · 10 adults · consecutive 4-week periods

Skin pH was higher with soap (p<0.01) and propionibacteria counts rose markedly, showing how cleanser pH shifts skin flora.

Korting HC et al. Acta Derm Venereol 1987;67(1):41–47 · PMID 2436413 ↗

Randomised controlled trialMild cleansing · infants

Alkaline soap vs syndets vs water

Open, randomised, controlled · 40 infants

Alkaline soap raised skin pH most (+0.45 vs +0.19 for water) and removed the most surface lipid; syndets were gentler.

Gfatter R et al. Dermatology 1997;195(3):258–262 · PMID 9407174 ↗

Randomised controlled trialGentle wash · newborn barrier

Baby wash vs water only

Assessor-blinded, randomised non-inferiority · 307 newborns · 14–28 days

The wash was non-inferior to water on TEWL (12.8 vs 12.5 g/m²/h) and gave higher skin hydration (p=0.022).

Lavender T et al. J Obstet Gynecol Neonatal Nurs 2013;42(2):203–214 · PMID 23421327 ↗

Randomised controlled trialWash routine · atopic skin

Frequent bathing + emollient vs infrequent bathing

Randomised, single-blind crossover · 42 children · 2 × 2 weeks

Eczema severity (SCORAD) fell by 21 points more with frequent bathing followed by moisturiser.

Cardona ID et al. J Allergy Clin Immunol Pract 2020;8(3):1014–1021 · PMID 31733336 ↗

Randomised controlled trialDry skin · glycerin vs urea

Glycerin cream vs urea-saline cream

Randomised, double-blind · 197 adults with atopic dermatitis · 30 days

Both relieved dryness equally; the glycerin cream caused less stinging (10% vs 24% moderate–severe).

Lodén M et al. Acta Derm Venereol 2002;82(1):45–47 · PMID 12013198 ↗

Controlled trialDry skin · body lotion

Glyceryl glucoside, NMF and ceramide lotion vs vehicle

Double-blind, vehicle-controlled studies · 169 adults · 2 weeks

Hydration rose and TEWL fell significantly vs vehicle and untreated skin; the richer lotion still moisturised 6 days after stopping.

Weber TM et al. J Clin Aesthet Dermatol 2012;5(8):29–39 · PMID 22916312 ↗

Randomised controlled trialHydration · glycerin

20% glycerol emollient vs vehicle

Double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomised · 24 adults with atopic dermatitis · 4 weeks

Skin hydration rose vs vehicle from week 1 (p<0.0001) and barrier function (TEWL) improved by week 4.

Breternitz M et al. Skin Pharmacol Physiol 2008;21(1):39–45 · PMID 18025807 ↗

Randomised controlled trialBarrier · ceramides

Ceramide cream vs hydrophilic cream, split-body

Double-blind, randomised · 24 adults with dry skin · 28 days + 7-day follow-up

Better hydration, lower TEWL and improved skin pH at every time point, maintained 7 days after stopping.

Lueangarun S et al. Dermatol Ther 2019;32(6):e13090 · PMID 31585489 ↗

Controlled trialFragrance · mood

Ambient orange or lavender scent vs music vs control

Controlled four-group trial · 200 adults

Both scents lowered anxiety and improved mood compared with no scent.

Lehrner J et al. Physiol Behav 2005;86(1–2):92–95 · PMID 16095639 ↗

These are published studies on ingredients, methods and routines, not on products we have made. A finished product needs its own testing before a claim goes on pack, and we design that study with you. See the full evidence library

FAQ

Body wash & body care questions.

Can a body wash really moisturise?

Yes, if it is built to leave oils or humectants on skin after rinsing. We prove it by measuring hydration after washing, compared with a standard cleanser.

Can you make a sulphate-free body wash that still foams well?

Yes. We combine mild primary surfactants with co-surfactants and foam boosters, then test foam volume and creaminess against your benchmark.

Can you develop a wash for babies or sensitive skin?

Yes. We use mild surfactant systems at skin-friendly pH, minimal fragrance or none, and test on sensitive-skin panels. Published trials show gentle washes can be as kind to newborn skin as water.

Do you create the fragrance?

We work with fragrance-house partners to brief, select and test a scent, or use one you already have, checked against IFRA limits and allergen labelling rules.

Who owns the formula?

You do. We sign an NDA before you share your brief, and the formula and technical file are handed over at the end of the project.
