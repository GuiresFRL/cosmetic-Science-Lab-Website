Zinc PCA: what the evidence shows for oily skin, blemishes and scalp

Zinc PCA is a staple of oil-control and blemish-care products. CSL formulation scientists found no trial of it on its own; the human data come from multi-active products, so claims need to stay modest.

By CSL Formulation team · Evidence checked by CSL scientists

Ingredient guideEvidence: Limited

At a glance

What it is
The zinc salt of pyrrolidone carboxylic acid (PCA), a natural part of the skin's moisturising factor
Main benefits
Claimed: less visible shine and oiliness; a supporting role in blemish and oily-scalp formulas
Levels tested in trials
1% in the one vehicle-controlled study found (with 4% niacinamide and 3% of a vitamin B6 derivative); usually not disclosed
Time to results
2 to 6 weeks in the multi-active studies found
Watch for
No trial of zinc PCA alone; sebum results are inconsistent; claims should rest on tests of the finished product

01What is it?

Zinc PCA combines zinc with PCA, a moisture-binding molecule that occurs naturally in the skin. It is used in toners, serums, cleansers, moisturisers and scalp products for oily and blemish-prone skin. It is almost always used alongside other actives, such as niacinamide or salicylic acid.

02What it does

Zinc has antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects and may reduce sebum, according to reviews of zinc in acne; PCA helps hold moisture. The expected visible benefit is less shine and calmer-looking skin. In the human studies found, zinc PCA's own share of any benefit cannot be separated from the other ingredients, and measured sebum did not consistently fall.

03The evidence

Strength of human evidenceLimited: no controlled trial of zinc PCA alone; human data come from small multi-active product studies, most without a control group

CSL verdict

Zinc PCA is a reasonable, well-tolerated supporting ingredient in oil-control, blemish and scalp formulas, but no trial has tested it on its own, and measured sebum fell in only one of the studies found. Keep claims to the finished product and to what was measured, for example “helps reduce the look of shine” or “helps keep skin looking clearer”, and do not present zinc PCA as a proven sebum-reducer by itself.

04How CSL formulation scientists use it

  • ☐Zinc PCA is water-soluble and easy to add to toners, gels, emulsions and shampoos; the one disclosed trial level was 1%. CSL formulation scientists check compatibility with thickeners and chelating agents, which can bind zinc.
  • ☐It is usually combined with better-evidenced actives such as niacinamide, salicylic acid or, on the scalp, piroctone olamine, so claims should reflect the whole formula.
  • ☐Sebum and shine claims should be supported by instrumental testing (for example Sebumeter readings) on the finished product, because published sebum results are inconsistent.
  • ☐It was well tolerated in all the studies found, which makes it a practical choice for leave-on and rinse-off products for oily skin and scalp.

05Regulatory and claims

No specific restriction for zinc PCA was confirmed during this review. CSL regulatory specialists confirm the status in each target market, including any rules on zinc compounds, and keep claims cosmetic: “helps reduce the look of shine” or “helps reduce visible flakes”, not “treats acne” or “treats seborrhoeic dermatitis”.

06Questions brands ask

Does zinc PCA reduce oil on its own?

That has not been shown. No trial of zinc PCA alone was found, and in the multi-active studies sebum fell in one, did not change in another and rose in a third.

Does zinc PCA help with dandruff or an oily scalp?

A small uncontrolled 2025 study of scalp products with zinc PCA, salicylic acid and piroctone olamine found less flaking and greasiness over 4 weeks. The anti-dandruff actives probably did most of the work.

Can zinc PCA be combined with other actives?

Yes. It is usually paired with niacinamide or salicylic acid. Watch for chelating agents in the formula, which can bind zinc.
